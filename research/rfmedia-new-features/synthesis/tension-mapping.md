# Tension Mapping: rfmedia-cli New Features and Enhancements

## Executive Summary

The deepest tensions across all research findings converge on three irresolvable axes: (1) whether rfmedia-cli should grow into a comprehensive library management platform or remain a focused copy/filter tool -- a question that seven of twelve identified contradictions trace back to; (2) whether Plex integration represents the tool's greatest asset or its greatest liability, where the unanimous consensus on risk coexists with the undeniable fact that half of recent development investment is Plex-specific; and (3) whether the tool should optimize for the power users who demand expression languages and radio-scheduling algorithms or the newcomers who abandon beets because it requires "a computer science PhD." These tensions cannot be dissolved through clever architecture alone -- they represent genuine zero-sum tradeoffs where choosing one side necessarily forecloses the other.

## Maximum Disagreement Points

### Tension 1: Feature Expansion as Survival vs. Feature Expansion as Suicide

- **Type**: Value/Conceptual
- **Description**: The research reveals a deep, unresolved split on whether rfmedia-cli's future depends on adding capabilities or consolidating what exists. Three personas (Futurist, Analogist, Archaeologist) collectively propose 20+ new features -- Essentia.js integration, content-addressable hashing, expression languages, separation rules, sidecar metadata, tiered verification, quality profiles, ListenBrainz integration, TUI modes, conversion caching, Merkle tree verification, and more. Two personas (Contrarian, Systems Thinker) argue with equal conviction that the tool's 17 completed features and 14+ commands already exceed what most CLI music tools attempt, and that every addition compounds interaction complexity at O(n^2).
- **Side A**: rfmedia-cli must expand to survive. The self-hosted music ecosystem has genuine gaps (no seamless pipeline, no radio-scheduling, no semantic search). Tools that stand still get replaced. The Analogist documents how every mature tool in adjacent domains (rsync, Bazel, Borg) evolved from simple operations to sophisticated state management. The Archaeologist identifies separation rules as a "missing link" that no competitor offers. The Futurist argues that MCP, AI analysis, and OpenSubsonic represent a once-in-a-decade convergence of enabling technologies.
- **Side B**: rfmedia-cli must consolidate or die. Songbird, Winamp under AOL, and iTunes all died or became hated because of feature accumulation. Beets' 80+ plugins created a 28x performance overhead, memory exhaustion, and a configuration complexity that its own maintainers acknowledge makes it unrecommendable for non-programmers. 16% of popular open-source projects are abandoned, with feature surface area as a contributing factor. The Unix philosophy -- "doing one thing well" -- is not just philosophy but a documented survival strategy.
- **Severity**: High
- **Resolvable?**: Partially. The crucible analysis proposes "dual-purpose features" (content hashing that simultaneously adds capability and improves infrastructure) as a resolution. But this is a design heuristic, not a principle that eliminates the tension. Each proposed feature still adds configuration surface, test requirements, documentation, and interaction pairs.
- **Insight**: This tension is the defining strategic question for rfmedia-cli. It cannot be resolved by external research -- only by empirical data about who actually uses the tool and which features they exercise. The absence of user telemetry is the single largest obstacle to resolving this debate.

### Tension 2: Plex Integration -- Deepening the Bet vs. Hedging the Bet

- **Type**: Stakeholder/Temporal
- **Description**: Every single persona raises concerns about Plex platform risk, making it the highest-consensus finding across all eight perspectives. Yet 9 of 18 recently completed issues are Plex-specific, and the Journalist documents that Plexamp remains "the gold standard for music playback quality and discovery." The tool's most differentiated features (Plex playlist sync, Plex collection management, rating-based filtering from Plex data) are precisely the ones most at risk.
- **Side A**: Plex integration is rfmedia-cli's core value proposition and should be deepened. Plex has the largest self-hosted media server install base. Plexamp's Sonic Analysis, Sonic Sage, and Sonic Adventure are unique competitive advantages that no other server offers. Plex reversed its music removal in July 2025 after user backlash, demonstrating responsiveness to its music community. Issue #44 (additional Plex features) serves the largest existing user segment.
- **Side B**: Plex is actively deprioritizing music (2.5% of usage). Pricing has increased sharply (lifetime from $119 to $250). Remote access is now paywalled ($1.99/month). JWT authentication migration will break existing token-based integrations. The Plex API returns "bizarrely formatted XML documents and refuses to give JSON." Navidrome's v0.60 WASM plugin system and OpenSubsonic compliance represent the ecosystem's future. The Historian documents the recurring pattern: open protocols outlast proprietary ones (CDDB to Gracenote to freedb to MusicBrainz).
- **Severity**: High
- **Resolvable?**: Partially. An abstraction layer that supports both Plex and OpenSubsonic is the consensus recommendation. But the Contrarian warns that Tomahawk died from "an endless maintenance burden" maintaining integrations with multiple services with a team of only 10 developers. Adding a second server backend doubles the integration maintenance surface. The choice is between single-platform risk and multi-platform maintenance burden -- there is no option that avoids both.
- **Insight**: The Plex tension reveals a deeper question about rfmedia-cli's identity: is it a "Plex power tool" or a "self-hosted music management tool"? These have different roadmaps, different audiences, and different competitive landscapes. The current codebase answers "Plex power tool" (9/18 features), but the research consensus says the future lies in server-agnostic operation.

### Tension 3: Power User Depth vs. Newcomer Accessibility

- **Type**: Stakeholder/Value
- **Description**: The Archaeologist and Analogist advocate for sophisticated capabilities that would make rfmedia-cli the most powerful CLI music tool ever built (foobar2000-style expression languages, radio-scheduling separation rules, quality profiles, content-addressable deduplication). The Negative Space explorer and Contrarian document that beets' own community admits the tool is "too hard to use" and would not "recommend Beets even for moderately technical users who are not professional programmers." The same capabilities that attract power users create the complexity ceiling that repels everyone else.
- **Side A**: Power users are rfmedia-cli's only viable audience. CLI tools self-select for technical users. The tools that lasted 20+ years (foobar2000, beets, MPD) all have steep learning curves and sophisticated capabilities. The Archaeologist identifies that "the query language is the product" -- every successful long-lived music tool had a powerful query/filter language at its core. Radio-scheduling separation rules would create a capability no competing tool offers.
- **Side B**: The people who most need music management tools are precisely those least likely to use CLI-based solutions. Decision paralysis from "too many options and metadata" is a documented adoption barrier. The Negative Space persona notes that "the tools capable of managing music well require technical expertise that excludes most potential users." CLI design guidelines emphasize that "consumers are attracted to a large number of choices but when it comes to actually using the product, having fewer options makes it easier."
- **Severity**: High
- **Resolvable?**: No. Progressive disclosure (sensible defaults with optional advanced features) is repeatedly proposed as a resolution, but it is a mitigation, not a solution. The README still grows. The help text still grows. The configuration schema still grows. A potential user evaluating rfmedia-cli's documentation will see 14+ commands and growing -- and some percentage will conclude it is "too complex for their needs" regardless of how well defaults are designed. The Systems Thinker identifies this as the "Complexity-Adoption Tradeoff" -- an emergent property that feature addition can actually reduce the user base.
- **Insight**: This tension forces an explicit audience choice. rfmedia-cli cannot simultaneously be "the most powerful music library CLI" and "the easiest music library CLI." The Futurist's assessment that "local music libraries will grow in the enthusiast/collector segment" suggests optimizing for depth over breadth. But this makes the tool's addressable market definitionally small.

### Tension 4: AI Integration -- Transformative Leap vs. Premature Complexity

- **Type**: Temporal/Conceptual
- **Description**: The Futurist and Journalist present compelling evidence that AI music analysis has reached production accuracy (96-98.5% genre classification, 95% mood classification) and is transitioning "from experimental to mainstream." The Contrarian presents equally compelling evidence that music tech AI sits at the "Peak of Inflated Expectations," that beets Chromaprint extends processing times by 10-15x, and that Essentia.js APIs are explicitly unstable.
- **Side A**: AI analysis is the highest-impact near-term integration opportunity. Essentia.js provides TypeScript bindings for production-ready audio analysis. Mood, energy, BPM, and key analysis would "dramatically expand smart filter capabilities." Plexamp's Sonic Sage demonstrates consumer demand. Acoustic fingerprinting captures 52.1% of music recognition market share. Cyanite.ai has tagged 45 million songs.
- **Side B**: Academic accuracy benchmarks (96-98.5% on curated GTZAN dataset) overstate real-world performance on diverse personal libraries with edge cases (mashups, genre-bending artists, non-Western music). Bridge.audio's self-reported 80% for "relevant suggestions" is more realistic. Chromaprint extends import times by 10-15x, transforming quick operations into long-running batch jobs. Essentia.js APIs are not yet stable and backward compatibility is not guaranteed. "Adding AI analysis to rfmedia-cli would introduce heavy dependencies for features that most CLI users would rarely invoke."
- **Severity**: High
- **Resolvable?**: Partially. A staged approach is possible: start with mature, low-risk capabilities (BPM verification, acoustic fingerprinting for identification) before venturing into experimental territory (mood classification, instrumentation detection). But the staged approach still requires the Essentia.js dependency (AGPL license, WASM, TensorFlow models) for even basic functionality. The crucible analysis eliminates "invest heavily in AI/ML" as a strategy, but individual components survive as narrow, optional additions.
- **Insight**: The gap between academic AI accuracy and real-world utility on messy personal libraries is the least-quantified assumption in the entire research corpus. No persona provides empirical data on what happens when you run mood classification against a 50K-track library with bootlegs, DJ mixes, field recordings, and non-Western music alongside mainstream pop and rock. This unknown makes the AI debate fundamentally speculative on both sides.

### Tension 5: Deduplication -- Detection Safety vs. Deletion Danger

- **Type**: Trade-off/Conceptual
- **Description**: The word "deduplication" conflates a safe operation (detection: find and report duplicates) with a dangerous one (deletion: automatically remove them). The Analogist, Archaeologist, and Journalist advocate for fingerprint-based deduplication as a well-validated feature with "market validation." The Contrarian and Systems Thinker call it a "tar pit" where false positives at scale produce destructive, irreversible outcomes.
- **Side A**: Content-addressable fingerprinting is "the single most impactful capability" across all cross-domain patterns. AcoustID has 30M+ fingerprints. A Berklee study found 41% of "hidden duplicates" escape filename detection. digiKam proves the approach works at scale for similar media. Tag-based deduplication is unreliable because "a track's tags are wrong or missing, or if the same song is encoded in different containers."
- **Side B**: Even a 0.1% false positive rate means 50 wrongly identified "duplicates" in a 50K library. Cover versions are frequently flagged because "the underlying audio is the same" to fingerprinting algorithms. MIDI deduplication research found "unacceptably high rate of false positives in practical de-duplication." The fundamental ambiguity of what constitutes a "duplicate" (FLAC + MP3 of same track? Live vs. studio? Same track on different albums?) makes any automated system unreliable. Deduplication involves deletion -- the undo system becomes critical but may be pruned before users realize they need it.
- **Severity**: High
- **Resolvable?**: Yes, if the feature is explicitly scoped as detection-only, never automated deletion. The Analogist's Nix package manager analogy provides the model: "present them as version conflicts with resolution options" rather than automatically deleting. Content hashing infrastructure (SHA-256 for byte-identical files) is unambiguously safe. Acoustic fingerprinting for "similar but not identical" detection is where the risk concentrates, and it must be advisory-only with confidence scores.
- **Insight**: The disagreement dissolves once the personas' implicit definitions are made explicit. The Analogist means "find and report duplicates." The Contrarian means "automatically delete duplicates." These are entirely different features with different risk profiles. Issue #38's design should explicitly state which it implements.

### Tension 6: Automation Efficiency vs. User Trust

- **Type**: Value/Trade-off
- **Description**: The Systems Thinker documents the "Automation Trust Spiral" -- a reinforcing loop where successful automation builds user trust, leading to larger batches with less review, silent error accumulation, and eventual catastrophic trust collapse. Multiple tools (OneTagger, Apple Music, Plex) have documented histories of corrupting user-curated metadata through automated "improvements." Yet the Analogist and Futurist propose deeper automation (content-based action caching, conversion caching, event-driven auto-organize, AI agent workflows via MCP).
- **Side A**: Automation with proper safeguards (content-addressable verification, tiered checks, Merkle tree validation) can be both efficient and reliable. Build systems like Bazel process millions of files with cryptographic verification and never produce incorrect outputs. The key insight is that automation and verification must be coupled: every automated operation includes embedded verification, not post-hoc checking.
- **Side B**: Audiophile communities express "deep skepticism about any software that modifies their files." Users report tools "automatically changing ID3v1/ID3v2 tags inside MP3 files without user instruction." Apple Music users complain that "Apple Music keeps messing with my library even after manually organizing and fixing tags." The beets community explicitly recommends importing albums one at a time for quality control, directly contradicting the value proposition of batch automation. The undo/rollback feature may give "false confidence" if the rollback mechanism has edge cases.
- **Side A rebuttal**: Build systems operate on deterministic inputs (source code) with well-defined outputs (binaries). Music libraries have subjective, ambiguous metadata where "correct" is a matter of opinion (genre classification, compilation handling, classical music conductor vs. composer attribution).
- **Severity**: Medium-High
- **Resolvable?**: Partially. The Bazel pattern (verify inputs and outputs cryptographically at every step) is sound for operations with objective correctness criteria (file integrity, format conversion). It does not apply to operations with subjective criteria (genre assignment, duplicate resolution, metadata normalization). The resolution is to automate what is objectively verifiable and require human judgment for what is subjective.
- **Insight**: Music library management is fundamentally different from build systems because "correct" output is partially subjective. Any automation feature must explicitly distinguish between objectively verifiable operations (file copied without corruption) and subjectively evaluable operations (this genre tag is appropriate) -- and only automate the former.

### Tension 7: Open Protocols vs. Pragmatic Platform Investment

- **Type**: Temporal/Value
- **Description**: The Historian documents a 30-year cycle where open community efforts are privatized, then forked back to open alternatives (CDDB to Gracenote to freedb to MusicBrainz; Subsonic open-source to closed-source to Navidrome). The conclusion is clear: "build on open data sources rather than proprietary ones." Yet rfmedia-cli's most mature and differentiated features are built on Plex's proprietary API.
- **Side A**: Open protocols (OpenSubsonic, MusicBrainz, ListenBrainz) represent the durable future. The Historian documents that community-maintained databases are "more efficient, flexible and antifragile" than commercial alternatives. Navidrome's rapid growth and WASM plugin system represent the ecosystem's trajectory. Plex's pricing increases (lifetime from $119 to $250) and feature restrictions (remote access paywalled) will drive users toward open alternatives.
- **Side B**: Plex has the largest install base today. Plexamp's sonic analysis capabilities (Sonic Sage, Sonic Adventure) are unmatched by any open-source alternative. Users already have Plex libraries. Migrating them is painful. The practical reality is that open protocols lag behind proprietary implementations in features and polish. Building for OpenSubsonic today means building for a smaller, less capable ecosystem.
- **Severity**: Medium-High
- **Resolvable?**: No -- this is a genuine timing problem. Investing in open protocols now means serving a smaller audience with fewer capabilities for the promise of long-term resilience. Investing in Plex now means serving the larger audience with richer features at the cost of platform dependency. The only mitigation is an abstraction layer, which doubles the maintenance burden -- itself a risk factor that killed Tomahawk.
- **Insight**: The 30-year pattern strongly suggests open protocols will win eventually. But "eventually" can mean 5-10 years. The question is whether rfmedia-cli can afford to invest in open protocols before they reach feature parity with Plex's music capabilities.

### Tension 8: Historical Wisdom vs. Modern Opportunity

- **Type**: Temporal/Conceptual
- **Description**: The Historian and Archaeologist identify patterns that endured for decades: composable tools, open protocols, powerful query languages, the Unix philosophy. The Futurist advocates for emerging technologies that the historical record cannot validate: AI analysis, MCP agent workflows, WASM distribution, spatial audio metadata. The tension is between building on what history proves works and reaching for what modern technology makes possible.
- **Side A**: The most durable features combine proven concepts with modern implementation. Radio-scheduling separation rules (40-year-old concept + modern SQLite) exemplify this. foobar2000-style expression languages (20-year-old concept + modern TypeScript parsing libraries) exemplify this. MPD sticker databases (20-year-old concept + existing SQLite infrastructure) exemplify this. History validates that composable tools, open data, and query languages outlast everything else.
- **Side B**: History also shows that tools that fail to adopt new paradigms become irrelevant. If AI-powered metadata analysis becomes table stakes (as the Journalist reports Plexamp's Sonic Sage and Spotify's Smart Filters suggest), a tool without it will be perceived as outdated. If MCP becomes the standard for tool interaction, tools without MCP support will be excluded from AI agent workflows. The Futurist notes rfmedia-cli already has MCP support -- maintaining this advantage requires continued investment.
- **Severity**: Medium
- **Resolvable?**: Yes, through a filtering heuristic: adopt modern technologies that extend historical patterns (AI-derived metadata feeding into the proven query-language paradigm) and reject those that create new paradigms (AI-agent-driven library reorganization replacing user-defined rules). The test is whether the new technology serves as input to existing composable systems or demands its own subsystem.
- **Insight**: The most durable features across the entire research corpus are those where a decades-old concept meets a modern implementation. Separation rules (radio scheduling from 1979 + SQLite from 2000), sidecar metadata (MPD stickers from 2003 + JSON from 2001), expression languages (foobar2000 title formatting from 2002 + TypeScript parser libraries from 2020s). The Futurist's AI and MCP proposals lack this historical grounding, making them higher risk.

## Stakeholder Tensions

### rfmedia-cli Developer vs. rfmedia-cli Users

The developer faces a maintenance burden that grows with every feature. The users want more capabilities. The Contrarian notes that "every feature is a maintenance liability" and that the current 17 completed features and 9 open issues already represent substantial surface area for a solo or small-team project. The Systems Thinker documents that "feature interaction complexity grows as O(n^2)." But the users (inferred from the Negative Space and Journalist findings) want library health dashboards, listening-informed curation, metadata reconciliation, and collection gap analysis. This is the classic maintainer-burnout tension.

### Audiophile Collectors vs. Media Server Operators

The Systems Thinker identifies these as distinct user segments with conflicting needs. Audiophile collectors (10-50K tracks, FLAC-centric) want "zero data loss, granular control, and transparency over every operation." Media server operators (20-100K tracks, mixed formats) want "minimal time spent managing, maximize content availability, set-and-forget workflows." Features that serve audiophiles (deep verification, sidecar metadata for tag preservation, non-destructive operations) conflict with features that serve operators (aggressive automation, bulk sync, watch mode). The audiophile demands manual confirmation; the operator demands automation. These are not different points on a spectrum -- they are orthogonal requirements.

### Plex Inc. vs. rfmedia-cli

Plex's incentives are to monetize remote access, focus engineering on video content (97.3% of usage), and reduce the maintenance burden of music features. rfmedia-cli's value depends on Plex's music API remaining functional and stable. These incentives are structurally misaligned. Plex has no contractual obligation to maintain API stability for third-party tools. The JWT migration, pricing increases, and music deprecation/un-deprecation cycle all demonstrate that Plex's decisions are driven by its own commercial logic, not by the needs of tools that build on its API.

### Present Users vs. Future Users

Current rfmedia-cli users have built workflows around Plex integration. Future users may come from the Navidrome/OpenSubsonic ecosystem. Building for future users (server abstraction layer) risks alienating current users (breaking changes, divided attention). Building for current users (deeper Plex features) risks missing the ecosystem shift. The Journalist documents that Navidrome's v0.60 represents a major architectural leap with WASM plugins and OpenSubsonic compliance -- suggesting the future user base may be substantial.

## Value Tensions

### Stability vs. Innovation

The Contrarian and Systems Thinker prioritize stability (performance benchmarks, test coverage, tag preservation). The Futurist, Analogist, and Archaeologist prioritize innovation (new capabilities, cross-domain patterns, forgotten techniques). These values cannot be simultaneously maximized because development time is finite. The crucible analysis resolves this by ranking stability first (H2) and innovation second (H4), but this is a prioritization, not a resolution -- both camps would claim the other's priority should be higher.

### Composability vs. Integration

The Unix philosophy (small tools that do one thing well) conflicts with the ecosystem integration vision (rfmedia-cli as the "glue" between acquisition, tagging, serving, and analytics). The Historian documents that composable tools outlast monolithic ones. The Negative Space persona documents that the ecosystem's fragmentation is its fundamental problem and only integration can solve it. A tool cannot simultaneously be minimal-and-composable and comprehensive-and-integrated. The Contrarian warns that Tomahawk's attempt to integrate multiple services killed it; the Negative Space persona argues that fragmentation without integration leaves every user stuck with manual workflows.

### Data Preservation vs. Data Modification

Audiophile communities "express deep skepticism about any software that modifies their files." Yet metadata enrichment (auto-tagging, genre classification, BPM detection, loudness normalization) requires writing to files. The Archaeologist's sidecar metadata proposal (write user data to SQLite, never touch files) partially resolves this, but some features inherently require file modification (format conversion writes new files, tag preservation during conversion requires writing tags to converted files). The philosophical split between "my files are sacred" and "my files need enrichment" runs deep in the self-hosted music community.

### Automation Speed vs. Curation Quality

The Systems Thinker's Reinforcing Loop R2 (Automation Trust Spiral) and the beets community's recommendation to import albums one at a time represent fundamentally incompatible approaches to the same problem. Watch mode, sync mode, and batch operations exist to save time. One-at-a-time import exists to ensure quality. These are zero-sum: every minute spent reviewing is a minute not saved by automation. No verification architecture fully resolves this because some quality dimensions (genre appropriateness, compilation handling) are subjective.

## Trade-off Tensions

### Performance at Scale vs. Feature Richness

Every proposed feature that touches the SQLite database (content hashing, fingerprint storage, sticker metadata, deduplication state, conversion caching, history expansion) adds per-track overhead. The beets cautionary tale (28x overhead at 10K tracks from N+1 queries) demonstrates that database operations compound. rfmedia-cli uses better-sqlite3 (synchronous, fast) which avoids some of Python's issues, but the fundamental tradeoff remains: more data stored per track means slower operations over all tracks. The Systems Thinker documents the causal chain: "Database grows further -> Performance degrades further." Without performance benchmarks (issue #57, still low priority), this tension is unquantified.

### Multi-Server Support vs. Maintenance Sustainability

Adding OpenSubsonic as a second server backend doubles the API integration surface. The Contrarian warns that Tomahawk died from maintaining integrations with a dozen services. The Futurist argues the growth vector is worth the cost. The irreconcilable element: rfmedia-cli has fewer developers than Tomahawk did, yet the proposal asks it to maintain integrations with more APIs. The abstraction layer creates its own maintenance burden (least-common-denominator API surface, adapter bugs, version skew between backends).

### Deduplication Accuracy vs. Deduplication Safety

Acoustic fingerprinting inherently trades precision against robustness. Higher sensitivity catches more duplicates but generates more false positives. Lower sensitivity misses duplicates but avoids false deletions. No threshold eliminates both risks simultaneously. The ISMIR paper cited by the Systems Thinker states this formally: "Boosting robustness typically leads to an increase of wrong identifications (false positives)." This is a mathematical constraint, not an engineering limitation.

### Configuration Expressiveness vs. Configuration Simplicity

The Archaeologist proposes a foobar2000-style expression language for file operations. The Negative Space persona documents that beets' YAML configuration is already a significant adoption barrier and that "decision paralysis from too many options and metadata" prevents organization. An expression language is maximally expressive and maximally complex. Sensible defaults mitigate but do not eliminate this: the language still exists, the documentation must explain it, and users must understand its syntax when defaults are insufficient.

## Key Insights

1. **The absence of user telemetry is the single most consequential gap in the research.** Every prioritization debate -- feature expansion vs. consolidation, Plex deepening vs. diversification, power users vs. newcomers -- could be substantially resolved by knowing who actually uses rfmedia-cli, how large their libraries are, which commands they use, and where they abandon workflows. The Contrarian, Systems Thinker, and Negative Space all independently identify this gap. Without usage data, the entire strategic debate rests on theory and analogical reasoning rather than evidence.

2. **Seven of twelve contradictions trace back to whether rfmedia-cli should expand or consolidate.** This volume indicates the question is genuinely unresolved and is the defining strategic tension. The crucible analysis's recommended sequence (H2 foundation -> H4 differentiation -> H1 risk mitigation -> selective H6) is the strongest available resolution, but it remains theoretical without user validation.

3. **Plex platform risk is the only finding where all eight personas agree.** The unanimous consensus that Plex integration is a strategic risk -- despite half of recent development being Plex-specific -- creates an urgent need for architectural preparation (abstraction layer design) even if the migration trigger (Plex API instability) has not yet occurred.

4. **The most durable features combine decades-old concepts with modern implementations.** Across all personas, the features that survive the most scrutiny are those grounded in historically proven patterns: radio-scheduling separation rules (1979 + SQLite), MPD-style sidecar metadata (2003 + JSON), foobar2000-style expression templates (2002 + TypeScript parsing). Features without historical precedent (AI mood classification, MCP agent workflows, TUI modes) face substantially more disconfirming evidence.

5. **The deduplication debate is a proxy for a deeper philosophical divide between advisory and autonomous tool design.** Should rfmedia-cli tell users what to do (detection, reporting, health dashboards) or do things for users (automated deletion, auto-tagging, event-driven reorganization)? The audiophile stakeholders want advisory. The media server operators want autonomous. This tension permeates every feature decision beyond deduplication.

6. **The "beets problem" -- where a powerful tool becomes its own greatest barrier -- is the most likely failure mode for rfmedia-cli.** Eight of eight personas reference beets, drawing contradictory lessons. Beets validates the CLI music management concept while simultaneously documenting every pitfall: N+1 queries, plugin fragility, configuration complexity, import tedium. rfmedia-cli's technology stack (TypeScript, better-sqlite3) avoids some specific beets failures, but the structural risks (feature accumulation, maintenance burden, adoption ceiling) are language-agnostic.

7. **The irreconcilable tensions demand explicit audience and identity choices that no amount of architectural cleverness can avoid.** rfmedia-cli cannot be simultaneously: the most powerful CLI music tool AND the most accessible; a Plex power tool AND a server-agnostic platform; a focused copy/filter tool AND a comprehensive library management system; optimized for audiophile curation AND media server automation. These choices constrain every downstream technical decision, and they must be made consciously rather than emerging accidentally from ad-hoc feature additions.
